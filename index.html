Wolters Kluwer AU

Re-inventing accounting software for the tax professional

Strategic Vision UX Research Stakeholder Influence Design System

My Role

Sole designer on the project, working in close partnership with the VP of UX. I owned the full scope, from initial product audit and user research synthesis through to journey mapping, concept design, and a fully interactive Figma prototype. I presented the final vision directly to the product and strategy leadership team to secure organisational buy-in.

Background

CCH iFirm is a professional accounting platform used daily by tax and accounting professionals across Australia. Despite its critical role in their work, the product had accumulated years of technical and design debt: its interface had barely evolved while user expectations and workflow complexity had grown significantly. A design system refresh was underway, but the deeper problem was structural: the product wasn't built around how accountants actually work.

Problem

Accountants are under constant time pressure, yet the software was actively slowing them down. Rather than enabling efficient, proactive work, it forced users into reactive, manual patterns:

  • Constant context switching: Simple tasks required jumping between multiple tools and manually copying and pasting data: work the software should have been doing automatically.
  • Reactive rather than proactive: The product gave users no foresight into their workload. Accountants were always catching up, never ahead.
  • No feedback loop: There was no mechanism to capture, analyse, or act on user pain points, making it impossible to prioritise improvements or build a coherent roadmap.
  • Fragmented strategy: Multiple regional product instances meant there was no shared vision for where the product was going, leaving the team unable to align on a path forward.

This combination of surface-level usability issues and deeper structural problems created a rare opportunity: rather than patching the existing product, we had the chance to step back and reimagine it from the ground up.

Process

I began with a thorough audit of the existing product, systematically documenting inconsistencies, friction points, and usability failures. This gave us a clear-eyed view of the current state before any design work began.

The most critical insight from this audit was that the product's structure didn't reflect how accountants actually work. Before designing anything, we needed to map their real workflow. Over several weeks in Miro, I worked closely with the VP of UX to build a comprehensive, highly detailed journey map covering every step an accountant takes from day to day. This became the single most important artefact of the project: the foundation every subsequent design decision was built on.

With the journey map established, I moved into defining the navigational framework, ensuring the product's structure mirrored the natural flow of an accountant's work rather than forcing them to adapt to the software. I explored multiple structural approaches, conducted desk research across professional SaaS products, and iterated in Figma using our existing design system before making the deliberate decision to push beyond it and define a future-state vision for the product and its design language.

Solution

The final deliverable was a fully interactive Figma prototype representing a complete reimagining of CCH iFirm, built around the accountant's natural workflow rather than legacy product structure. It covered:

  • A proactive navigation framework structured around the steps accountants actually take, surfacing what needs attention rather than waiting for users to go looking for it
  • Automated workflow patterns that eliminated the manual, repetitive tasks users were doing themselves: copy-pasting, tool switching, status checking
  • Logic flows for all core patterns: onboarding, account management, help, e-commerce, and trial-to-subscribe, each grounded in the journey map
  • A future-state design vision that went beyond the existing component library to establish what the product and its design system could become

Impact

The outcome of this project wasn't a shipped product: it was organisational alignment. The presentation to product and strategy leadership was met with strong endorsement, directly resulting in the formation of a dedicated Global Platform Team. I was appointed as the lead designer for this team, tasked with building the vision for Wolters Kluwer's next generation of global accounting software. The concepts developed in this project became the foundational backbone for that initiative.

Wolters Kluwer AU

Exploring integrated AI tax agents

AI Product Design Discovery Workflow UX

Background

Every year, Wolters Kluwer runs the Global Innovation Awards, an internal programme paired with a CodeGames hackathon, that brings cross-functional teams together to pitch new ideas for improving internal processes or customer-facing experiences. This project, CCH CiteGenie, was created by the APAC tax and accounting team in the year ChatGPT went mainstream, when everyone was asking the same question: how could this technology actually improve our customers' workflow? I was brought onto the idea early for the user research and design perspective, as it moved through the Awards' staged process, each stage going deeper into the solution, its potential revenue, and the problem it solved.

The problem

We started from an assumption rather than validated fact: tax professionals conducting research were dealing with information overload, tight time constraints, and a research process that was inherently cumbersome, all while accuracy and regulatory compliance left no room for error. CiteGenie was proposed as an AI-powered research assistant to tackle these pain points directly. But an assumption isn't a business case, so before the idea could become a credible entrant into the Innovation Awards, we needed evidence.

My role

I led the research and design validation work that turned the idea into something the Awards judges, and the business, could actually evaluate: designing and running a fake-door experiment and an in-platform survey to test real interest, contributing to the lean canvas that framed the opportunity, and designing the interactive Figma prototype that became the reference for the dev team's working demo.

The fake-door experiment, launched inside CCH iKnow to gauge genuine interest in an AI research assistant, plus the flow analytics used to track completion against a benchmark.

The process

To validate demand quickly, we ran a fake-door experiment directly inside CCH iKnow, our live tax research product: an early-access landing page pitching the AI assistant, with a simple email capture as the call to action. Email CTAs like this typically convert at 2–3%; ours completed at roughly 5%, close to double the benchmark, which gave us a genuine signal rather than a hopeful one.

Alongside that, we mapped the opportunity on a lean canvas (problem, solution, unique value proposition, market size, channels, and revenue streams) to understand the idea from every angle a judge or stakeholder would ask about.

The CCH CiteGenie Lean Canvas: problem, solution, unique value proposition, market sizing, and revenue projections used to pressure-test the idea for the Innovation Awards pitch.

Given how quickly the hackathon-style Awards process moves, we couldn't wait for a fully-scoped build, so I designed a working prototype in Figma that the dev team used as a direct reference to build an actual functioning demo for the final round. Beyond the core integrated experience, we also scoped out third-party integrations (Outlook, Teams, and even competitor software) to make the workflow seamless wherever our customers already were, leaning on the strength of our tax and accounting content to justify reaching beyond our own platform.

Early prototype explorations of the chat-based research flow, handed to the dev team as the reference for the working hackathon demo.

Outcomes

The idea made it all the way to the final round of the Innovation Awards, ultimately placing second. While it didn't move forward as a funded initiative, the process did what good discovery is supposed to do: it got every stakeholder in the room genuinely excited about what AI could do for our customers, and opened eyes across the business to what the future of our platform and content could look like.

Wolters Kluwer Global

The path to a first-ever global design system

Design Systems Component Library Global Scale

Background

Wolters Kluwer is a global leader in professional information, software, and services, operating across numerous distinct market segments with a product portfolio built up significantly through decades of acquisitions. That history left a fragmented landscape: disparate user experiences and inconsistent brand representation from one product to the next, with no shared visual language or interaction framework tying any of it together. The initiative to build the organisation's first-ever comprehensive design system set out to change that.

A snapshot from the early audit: the same kinds of interfaces (search, document viewers, content trees) rebuilt independently across different Wolters Kluwer products, with no shared foundation between them.

The approach

As a core member of the dedicated design system team, my initial focus was discovery and strategic planning: running workshops to define scope, identify key components and patterns, and work within existing brand guidelines for icon, colour, and type. We started with a thorough audit of current Wolters Kluwer applications, documenting existing patterns, components, and pages in search of both commonalities and inconsistencies.

That audit surfaced a crucial distinction that shaped everything after it: two fundamentally different product types, "Research" and "Workflow," distinguished primarily by their navigation pattern: a horizontal, "Fixed" chrome for Research products, versus an expandable left-hand, "Fluid" chrome for Workflow products. This distinction became the backbone of the system's initial component list.

The two foundational chrome patterns the system was built around: expandable "Fluid" navigation for Workflow products (left) and horizontal "Fixed" navigation for Research products (right).

Over several months (evolving into years, since a design system is a living product, not a one-off deliverable), we designed and developed components in agile sprint cycles, shipping Version 1, then Version 2, and now progressing through Version 3. Along the way we refined our own toolchain: moving from Sketch to Figma for design, and establishing ZeroHeight for documentation in place of Storybook, with every environment linked and kept in sync.

The design system's Figma libraries at scale today: hundreds of synced components across Fundamentals, Components, and Patterns, alongside product-specific libraries built on top of them.

My role

Building a design system is inherently a team effort, and this one was no exception, but my specific contributions included:

  • Designing reusable components and writing their documentation
  • Research and analysis on patterns, pages, and components across products org-wide
  • Helping establish which components, patterns, and pages needed to be built, and in what order
  • Attending cross-functional global workshops to shape strategy, vision, and prioritisation
  • Annotating designs and running feedback loops with development and other stakeholders
  • Collaborating with other designers, product managers, technical leads, and stakeholders across the org

Outcomes

The design system has become an indispensable tool for both design and development: designers work directly in Figma to integrate components, while developers pull the matching React or Angular code, dramatically accelerating workflows on both sides and freeing up time for user-centric problem-solving instead of rebuilding the same patterns from scratch. Complete consistency across complex page-level patterns is still an ongoing effort: the component library solved brand and UI consistency, but applying those components into fully standardised page layouts remains a live focus for future iterations.

Wolters Kluwer NL / AU / NZ

Enabling efficient tax, accounting & legal research

Research Tools Information Architecture Professional UX

Background

IntelliConnect, our legacy legal and tax research product, served more than 50,000 users across the APAC region, loved by many of them, but also showing its age. Launched years earlier without design or user experience in mind, it had accumulated serious technical debt, and a global company directive put us on a deadline to sunset it entirely by the end of 2023.

It wasn't the only legacy product in the mix. A separate, simpler platform called iKnow had launched in 2016 for small accounting firms (plus a New Zealand variant), and in 2019 a companion product, Pinpoint, launched for smaller legal firms. Both were loved by their target audience for their modern, fast, cross-device experience, but larger firms who tried them stayed on IntelliConnect. By 2020 we had four fragmented products in market, a small development team, a couple of product members, and a single UX designer: me. This became project "Tahi" (Māori for "one") with one goal: sunset all four legacy platforms and replace them with a single, future-proof product that actually solved our customers' pain points.

My role

I owned UX end-to-end for the project: building the research plan and scripts, running and analysing customer interviews (including calls in the middle of the night to cover an 8-hour timezone gap while working remotely from the Netherlands for the APAC business), building the affinity wall and research taxonomy, designing the high-fidelity Figma prototype, and running usability testing. Around 18 months in, a junior UX designer joined to take on production design and day-to-day dev collaboration, freeing me to focus on strategy, high-level concepts, and solving problems as they surfaced during the beta programme.

Contextual inquiry and research

First order of business: understand the actual customer pain points, not assumed ones. Early 2020 made that harder than usual: remote research became the only option, so I combined efforts with product members and our in-house editorial team to recruit and interview more than 10 customers across Australia and New Zealand, spanning IntelliConnect, iKnow, and Pinpoint users. I built the research plan and scripts, ran the interviews (delegating recruitment to our commercial managers, who held the customer relationships), and synthesised everything into a full contextual inquiry analysis.

The affinity wall built from over 10 contextual inquiry interviews across Australia and New Zealand, mapping how customers actually search and browse for content.

The research settled on two dominant pathways customers take to find what they need: searching directly, or browsing to it, and made clear that both were the sharpest pain points to solve first. After building the affinity wall, I ran a full-day (genuinely all-nighter, timezone-wise) workshop to "walk the wall" with the team, brainstorm design directions, and pressure-test which concepts were actually working.

Prototyping and usability testing

From there we built a clickable high-fidelity prototype in Figma and took it back to the same customers, plus new ones, through both moderated interviews and unmoderated Maze testing, comparing the new design directly against the two products it was meant to replace, iKnow and Pinpoint, across 8 core research tasks.

Usability testing results across iKnow and Pinpoint testers: task completion, ease-of-use scores, and verbatim feedback that directly shaped the new information architecture.

In parallel, I set up a living research taxonomy in Dovetail, our research repository, tagging every insight by user role, organisation size, region, and sentiment, so any stakeholder could search across the full body of customer evidence, not just what I happened to remember. That taxonomy is still growing today, now folding in everything gathered since launch. The product manager and I also presented our discovery and design approach directly to the broader organisation and executive team.

The full information architecture: every legacy IntelliConnect, iKnow, Pinpoint, and NZ content path mapped and restructured into a single new platform.

A product-led transition strategy

The business risk wasn't just building the right product: it was moving 50,000+ users onto it without losing them, or overwhelming customer success and sales in the process. We designed a product-led transition: banners, redirection, in-product notifications, onboarding flows, tooltips, and help content, largely built and owned by the product manager, product owner, and me. We rolled it out in four phases (an invite-only Early Bird beta, a General beta open to all IntelliConnect users, automatic redirection with an optional switch-back, and finally sunsetting the legacy platforms), deliberately shipping an MVP rather than waiting for perfection, so we could gather real signal earlier.

We hit our Early Bird launch on 23 June, with the critical MVP features in place. The feedback loop this created (real customers, in their real day-to-day workflow, giving us satisfaction scores, ratings, and direct feedback) taught us more than any research phase before it, surfacing behaviours we'd never uncovered in the contextual inquiry.

Outcomes

Taking everything we'd learned across every product's strengths and weaknesses, we shipped something several customers described as "the perfect baby between iKnow and IntelliConnect." With the general beta live, redirection flows in place, and future releases already designed against a validated research base, CCH iKnowConnect replaced four fragmented legacy platforms with one, modular, reusing our global design system, and built to keep evolving without sliding back into technical debt.

ReadyTech

Building ReadyTech's first Design System: the strategy behind the start

Design Systems Strategy UI Audit 0 to 1 Cross-functional Buy-in

Where this stands today

This is an early-stage initiative, not a finished system. What follows is the audit that built the case for it, and the strategy guiding what gets prioritised, rather than a catalogue of shipped components.

The situation

ReadyTech's products had grown organically across several squads, each building UI independently. There was no shared component library, no documented patterns, and no single source of truth for what a button, a navigation panel, a header, or a table should look like or behave like from one product to the next. On the surface this looked like a design consistency problem. It wasn't just that: it was a delivery problem. Every time a component needed to change, engineers were finding and updating it by hand, wherever it happened to live.

A snapshot from the audit: buttons, navigation, headers and breadcrumbs captured side by side across products: the same components, styled and behaving differently in each one.

My role

Nobody had asked for a design system. Part of the job was building the argument for why ReadyTech needed one, starting with evidence rather than opinion. I led this from identifying the problem through to quantifying it via a full UI audit, and used that audit to shape both the pitch and what gets tackled first.

The approach: an audit, not a component library

Rather than jumping straight into building components speculatively, I audited every core UI pattern across ReadyTech's three products (buttons, navigation panels, headers, breadcrumbs, inputs, tables, modals, and more), cataloguing every variant in use, where it lived, and how consistent it was.

The findings were sharpest in the parts of the product people touch constantly. Buttons had different colours, hover effects, sizes and fonts depending on which product you were in. Navigation panels varied in font, sizing, colour and even positioning from one product to the next: the single most-used element on every page, and the least consistent. Headers were inconsistent across all three products, down to literally different heights (20px in one product, 44px in another, 56px in the third), undermining a consistent brand and entry point across the platform. Tables were the closest thing to a bright spot: internally consistent within Recruitment, but inconsistent within RWF and entirely absent from Ready Talent.

Each pattern was scored for severity and frequency: how disruptive the inconsistency was, and how often users would run into it. Buttons, navigation, and headers scored highest on both counts: they're not just inconsistent, they're inconsistent in the places users see and interact with the most. That combination is what turned "we should tidy up the UI" into a concrete, evidence-backed case for a design system.

This reframed the whole conversation. It's not about which component is quickest or easiest to build, especially now that AI can generate components fast once a decision is made. It's about where inconsistency is doing the most damage to the user's experience, weighed against the engineering effort already being burned maintaining that inconsistency by hand. That's the lens the strategy is built on.

The strategy from here

  • Prioritise by user impact, not build effort: the audit's severity and frequency scores, not ease of implementation, decide what gets tackled first. Navigation, headers, and buttons lead because they're foundational to every screen a user touches.
  • Engineering partnership on governance: establishing how components get contributed to, versioned, and maintained so the system doesn't drift the way the old UI did.
  • Adoption across squads: working directly with teams to migrate existing screens, rather than assuming a library alone will drive adoption.
  • Measurement, eventually: ReadyTech's product analytics maturity is still developing; once it's in place, tracking adoption and time saved becomes possible in a way it isn't yet.

Outcomes so far

It's still early. The honest measure of success right now isn't a metric. It's that ReadyTech has, for the first time, a documented, evidence-based view of exactly where inconsistency is hurting users most, and a clear strategy for what to fix first and why.

ReadyTech

Driving problem-solution fit: shifting the team from features to root causes

Discovery Strategy Problem Framing Cross-functional Influence

A note on measurement

ReadyTech's product analytics is still maturing, so the impact here is tracked qualitatively (through the requests that got reframed and the decisions that changed) rather than a dashboard of numbers. That's part of the story, not a gap in it.

The situation

Most feature development so far had been reactive, driven by four familiar triggers: a customer asking directly for something, a customer threatening to churn if it wasn't delivered, a competitor already shipping the feature, or a push to "add AI" to a product area. Each of these arrived pre-solutioned, the fix already specified, the brief already written, with little room left to ask whether that fix was actually the right one.

The risk in taking requests at face value like this is a familiar one: you can ship exactly what was asked for and still not solve the underlying problem, retain the customer only until the next threat, or ship an AI feature that adds noise rather than value, while missing a simpler, better-fitting solution the original request never considered.

Epic Consolidated Activity Feed in Ready People
PRD Shift Bidding Marketplace
PRD Manage by Exception Timesheets

Three real epic and PRD titles, pulled straight from the backlog. Each one names its solution before a problem statement exists anywhere in the ticket.

My role: introducing friction, deliberately

My job wasn't to say no to feature requests. It was to slow the handoff between "request" and "build" just enough to ask what problem this was actually trying to solve, for whom, and why this particular fix was the one being proposed. That meant working directly with Product and Engineering before a request became a committed piece of work.

The strategy

The shift wasn't a single research project. It was introducing continuous discovery as a habit, so that interrogating a request became a normal step before committing to it, not an occasional detour. In practice, this meant talking directly to the people raising requests and the users affected by them, looking at where the request sat in their actual workflow, and identifying what was genuinely going wrong before accepting the proposed fix as the answer.

The goal wasn't to slow teams down. It was to make sure we were building the right thing before we built anything at all. In practice, this meant creating a separate artefact for the reframe rather than editing the original ask: an opportunity canvas capturing the real problem, the users affected, and the underlying need, sitting alongside the original request rather than replacing it. In several cases this surfaced a different, better-fitting solution than the one originally proposed. Just as importantly, it began shifting the working relationship with squads: the default conversation moved from "build this feature" towards "what problem are we actually solving, and is this the best way to solve it".

Two reframes, in practice

Two of the three tickets above are worth walking through, because the gap between the title and what the opportunity canvas actually surfaced is the whole point.

The ticket said
"Shift Bidding Marketplace"
The canvas found

Underneath the feature name, the real problems were operational and financial: last-minute shift changes carried genuine compliance and budget risk, managers were burning effort just trying to remember who was even available, and the process felt opaque and unfair from the employee side. There was a real business case behind it too, deals lost to competitors who already had this capability, and retention risk with the customers who wanted it most. That reframing is why the eventual scope grew past a simple bidding board into a manager-controlled way to fill a shift with cost and compliance risk visible up front.

The ticket said
"Manage by Exception Timesheets"
The canvas found

The title implied hiding approved timesheets from view. What the canvas surfaced was a trust problem: managers didn't believe an automated approval without being able to see why it happened, so they kept manually cross-referencing rosters against clock-ins for every entry, approved or not. It carried its own measurable business case too, in lost sales and retention risk tied to the same gap. That reframing shaped an MVP that surfaces the reasoning behind every approval decision inline, and a way for admins to test their own rules against real historical data before trusting the system with anything live, rather than a dashboard that just quietly hides what's already been approved.

Two opportunity canvases run against tickets that already had a solution in the title. In both cases the canvas found a different, better-evidenced problem underneath, and the eventual build followed that, not the original ticket name.

From reframe to production

One of the two reframes above has already shipped. The inline exception-messaging work that came out of interrogating "Manage by Exception Timesheets" is live: instead of managers scanning every timesheet by hand, the system surfaces the reasoning behind each approval decision directly where they already work, and auto-approves the ones that don't need a human look. As of 1 July, 95 customers have it enabled, roughly 70% of evaluated timesheets are being auto-approved, and only 4.8% of those get reversed after the fact. That reversal rate is the number that actually matters here: it's an indirect but genuine signal that managers trust what the system is approving on their behalf, not just that the feature is switched on.

This rollout has also become one of the first places the design system's updated foundational colours replaced older, ad-hoc ones that had crept into the timesheet screens with no real reason to be there, part of the same broader colour migration now under way across the rest of the platform.

The shipped review flow: shifts that match the roster auto-approve silently, the ones that don't get flagged with the specific reason why, a variance in duration, a mismatch against the roster, rather than leaving a manager to spot it by eye.

The full set of timesheet status states rebuilt on the design system's foundational colours, replacing a set of older, ad-hoc colours that had accumulated in this part of the product with no real reason behind them.

Embedding it as a practice

  • Discovery before commitment: a lightweight check-in on the "why" before a request enters the roadmap, rather than after.
  • Direct conversations over assumptions: going to the source of a request (the customer or internal stakeholder) rather than relying on how it was relayed.
  • Reframing as a shared language: working with Product Managers so that questioning a request's framing became a normal, expected part of prioritisation conversations, not a challenge to their judgement.

Outcomes so far

Without mature product analytics in place yet, the clearest evidence of impact is qualitative: the two reframes above, and others like them, plus a shift in how confidently teams can now explain why they're building something, not just what.

ReadyTech

Redesigning rostering, from research to pilot

Roster Redesign Mobile & Desktop 0 to Pilot

Where this stands today

This one's still live work, so some specifics are kept general and a couple of the images below are from an AI-assisted interactive prototype rather than the shipped product. What follows is the real arc: the research, the redesign of the core roster experience, and the shift-filling feature that came out of it, heading into pilot.

The situation

ReadyTech's roster grid is the single most-loved part of the platform and the biggest time-saver customers name, but it's also carrying real friction. Useful features exist that most people never find. Cost and overtime risk aren't visible until the pay run proves it wrong. And when a shift falls through at the last minute, managers fall back on shadow IT: a WhatsApp group, a phone tree, whatever gets a warm body into the gap fastest, then manually cross-reference every volunteer against timesheets just to guess whether saying yes will accidentally trigger overtime. Meanwhile, on the other side of that same shift, frontline employees increasingly expect a faster, gig-like way to pick up extra hours than waiting on a text they might not see in time.

My role

I'm the product designer across the whole arc, research through pilot: I ran and synthesised the customer research, worked with product and engineering to define the problems worth solving first, designed the redesigned roster grid and a dedicated shift-filling flow across desktop and native mobile, built an AI-assisted interactive prototype to test and iterate the ideas quickly, mapped every notification the feature could send, and helped phase the delivery so nothing ships as a disruptive cutover for people who rely on this daily.

The research

Customer interviews on both sides of the problem, roster managers and frontline employees, settled a few things early. The base roster is loved and the biggest time-saver in the product, so this was always a re-skin and a re-layer, never a replacement. Useful capabilities already exist but go undiscovered. Cost and budget visibility is the strongest positive pull when it works, and the most frustrating when it's inconsistent, and reliability issues have already eroded enough trust that some managers keep a paper backup just in case. There's no proactive overtime warning at the point of rostering, so managers get surprised after the fact rather than warned before it happens. And appetite for a shift-bidding style feature is genuinely split: larger, cost-driven operators want it badly, smaller relationship-based teams don't want it at all, which is exactly why the manager stays in control of who gets assigned throughout, staff can express interest, but nothing auto-assigns behind their back.

The redesigned roster grid in the real product: unfilled shifts flagged directly on the grid, with a roster alert linking straight into best-fit candidates ranked by interest, availability and cost.

Designing across desktop and mobile

Managers work from the desktop roster they already live in, so the shift-filling flow needed to sit naturally inside it rather than as a bolted-on separate tool: select the open shift, and the system filters out anyone ineligible against hard constraints (missing qualifications, clashing rosters, approved leave), then ranks who's left against the things a manager actually weighs before offering it out, cost, and the risk of triggering immediate or cascading overtime. Employees needed the opposite: something built for a glance and a tap on a native mobile app, a push notification that turns into an accept, apply, or decline in as few steps as possible, competing directly with the immediacy of a text message.

Offering an open shift: eligible staff filtered against hard constraints, ranked against cost and overtime warnings, with a first-to-accept or manager-review offer method and a one-click broadcast to the whole eligible group.

Prototyping with AI

Rather than static comps, I built the later-stage flows as interactive, code-based prototypes using an AI-assisted prototyping workflow, grounded directly in the design system's real tokens and the actual research findings rather than built from a blank canvas. That let me test and iterate a flow in something much closer to real markup than a flat mockup, and hand engineering a working starting point rather than a set of disconnected screens.

Mapping every notification

A feature like this lives or dies on trust in its notifications. If an employee is offered a shift and never hears back, they won't open the app for the next one. So alongside the interaction design, I mapped every notification across every fill workflow, assign directly, first-to-accept, and manager review, who receives each one, when, and whether it's a push or an in-app message, down to edge cases like what a late acceptor sees versus a silent non-responder, and what happens the moment an offer expires.

The moment a shift is claimed: an immediate, unambiguous confirmation on mobile, closing the loop the same way a decline, expiry, or "someone else got it" state does.

Designed natively for both platforms: iOS (left) and Android (right) lock-screen notifications for the same shift events.

Phasing without breaking trust

Rostering is muscle memory and payroll is unforgiving, so nothing here ships as a forced cutover. Reliability issues people already distrust get fixed first, before anything new lands on top of numbers people don't believe. Lightweight in-product guidance surfaces the existing tools most people had never noticed. An insight layer, a metric strip and a needs-attention queue, lands on top of the existing grid so power users can ignore it entirely and keep working exactly as they do today. Only once that's proven does the redesigned grid go behind a reversible, sticky toggle with a handful of design-partner customers, classic view fully supported throughout. And the shift-filling feature itself gets piloted specifically with the segments that asked for it, off by default for the smaller, relationship-based teams that said plainly they didn't want it.

Outcomes so far

It's early for the shift-filling piece specifically, pre-pilot, so there's no usage data to report yet. But the problem is validated directly through research on both sides, the MVP is scoped, phased, and prototyped end to end, and the signal ahead of pilot has been strong: enough customer interest in trialling it that it's now moving from a phased plan into an actual pilot.
